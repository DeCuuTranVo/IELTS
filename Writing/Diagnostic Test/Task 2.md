## Essay

These days, a lot of students choose to pursue vocational careers. Many of them prefer spending more time on occupational courses than on regular academic subjects. This has led to debate over how their school time should be allocated. In my opinion, the best approach is to divide their time evenly, which offers the best of both worlds.

On the one hand, investing time early in occupational subjects benefits the students in multiple ways. One advantage is that students can get more professional training and occupational experience. It is obvious that a lot of trade skills demand more hands-on practice than theoretical knowledge. More time for in-depth practice essentially means higher expertise. For example, experienced cooks often develop a refined sense of taste, which cannot be learned from regular classes. Another advantage is that having the time to gain adequate expertise gives the students a competitive edge on the job market. In fact, a cook with three years of experience is more likely to get a job in well-known restaurants. 

On the other hand, a fair amount of time allocated for general knowledge acquisition is still crucial for those students. Fundamental knowledge taught in academic classes helps vocational workers understand how things work instead of blindly following manuals and procedures. For instance,  a baker needs to know the mechanism of fermentation and caramelization in order to control the flavor of the final products and derive new recipes. In addition, regular subjects equip students with literacy skills and a familiarity with written assessments. Those capabilities help students pass vocational licensing exams during their careers.

In conclusion, although students who intend to pursue vocational careers should receive substantial practical training, academic subjects remain essential to their long-term professional development. Schools should therefore provide a balanced combination of vocational and academic education.
